2. (Peeling the Mask: The Psychological Unravelling in Parasite

"You know what kind of plan never fails? No plan at all."  –Ki-taek
Pretend at the Expense of Collapse 
Bong Joon-ho's Parasite is often praised for its social commentary on class divide—but underneath its architecture of satire and suspense lies a more intimate, disturbing portrait of psychological erosion. Each character is, in essence, a shell—polished, curated,masked—until the film forces them to confront who they are beneath their personas.
In Parasite, everyone is pretending. The poor pretend to be rich; the rich pretend they're safe.But pretense is costly. It comes with the price of performance, suppression, and detachment from the raw parts of the self. Over time, the performance becomes unsustainable—and when it breaks, what's left is chaos. 
This essay explores how the central characters—the Kims and the Parks—lose their psychological shells. It is a study in adaptive behavior, shame, class-induced trauma, and the invisible labor of self-control. More than a story of social hierarchy, Parasite is about the mind's struggle to stay intact in a world that won't let people be seen for who they truly are.

The Kims: Performance as a Survival Mechanism
The Kim family—Ki-taek (father), Chung-sook (mother), Ki-woo (son), and Ki-jung (daughter)—begin the film as survivors. They are poor, but resourceful. Their daily life is marked by hypervigilance,improvisation, and emotional detachment, all of which are adaptive behaviors rooted in long-term poverty - often associated with heightened stress reactivity,executive function impairment, and identity diffusion. Over time, individuals begin to define themselves not by who they are, but by how they must adapt to survive.
Ki-woo (Kevin): The Delusion of Upward Mobility
When Ki-woo gets the tutoring job, he crosses a boundary—physical and psychological. He steps into a world that was never meant for him. But instead of resisting the illusion, he leans into it. He changes his name to Kevin, fabricates a university background, and plays the role with increasing confidence. This is imposter syndrome inverted: rather than feeling like a fraud, Kevin convinces himself that he belongs. He embodies the psychological defense of identification with the aggressor—aligning with the dominant class to avoid the pain of exclusion. But this is unsustainable. His fantasy of mobility eventually collapses when he's brutally attacked in the final act—a symbolic punishment for overreaching his class position. 
Ki-jung (Jessica): The Art of Emotional Detachment
Ki-jung is the most composed, and adaptable. She becomes "Jessica" the art therapist, delivering a performance so convincing it borders on psychopathy—not in the clinical sense, but in her lack of emotional hesitation. She is a study in compartmentalization, cutting off parts of herself to become what the environment demands. But her composure, too, is a shell. She dies in the final act, not because she was less intelligent, but because emotional repression does not equate to psychological safety. She is calm and detached, but her death reminds us that even the most controlled facades can't withstand systemic violence.
Ki-taek: Shame and the Slow Burn of Rage
Ki-taek is the most tragic. He starts as an affable, passive father, humiliated by his inability to provide, but always playing it off with jokes. He laughs through discomfort, a typical response to internalized shame. But the microaggressions from Mr. Park—especially around "smell"—chip away at this shell.
Psychological note: Shame, unlike guilt, is not about what one has done but about who one is. It corrodes the self from within and often turns outward as rage when triggered.
Ki-taek's breakdown is slow, quiet, and deeply human. By the end, when he stabs Mr. Park, it's not because of a single offense—it's the unbearable accumulation of being made to feel invisible, inferior, and inhuman.
The Parks: Detachment, Denial, and the Illusion of Safety
While the Kim family embodies the role of scarcity in emotional detachment, the Park family shows a different side of the same coin : how privilege can create its own forms of distance and performance. They may live above ground—literally and socially—but their psychological lives are no less fractured. Their privilege creates a bubble of detachment, a life so curated that they become emotionally dull, intellectually disengaged, and psychologically unprepared for disruption.
Mrs. Park: Infantilization and the Need to Be Managed
Mrs. Park is portrayed as naïve, overly trusting, and emotionally fragile. She relies heavily on others to manage her home, her children, and her emotional responses. Her psychological profile leans toward learned helplessness—not born from trauma, but from overprotection and emotional outsourcing. She is not incapable; she is unused to discomfort. Her wealth has insulated her from stress, weakening her emotional resilience. She is not malicious, but she is complicit. Her ability to remain passive is a form of denial that enables the status quo.
Mr. Park: Detachment and Dehumanization
Mr. Park is the ideal neoliberal patriarch—composed, successful, and emotionally distant. His psychological shell is built on disassociation : he does not see the people who serve him as real. His comments about Ki-taek's "smell"—always said when he thinks he can't be heard—reflect a deep-seated classist disgust. It's not personal to him; it's structural. He has trained himself not to look too closely at the lives beneath his feet. But this detachment costs him. He cannot anticipate danger because he does not understand desperation. His death is not random; it is the final result of emotional blindness
.
The Cellar: A Metaphor for the Repressed Mind
Suppose the Park house is a symbol of upper-class consciousness. In that case, the hidden basement represents everything the Parks refuse to acknowledge—the unconscious, the shadow self, the truth beneath the performance. 
Geun sae, the man living in the basement, is a ghost—not just literally, but psychologically. He embodies repression. He's what happens when someone is ignored long enough that they no longer belong to society, only to memory and fear. 
Psychological note: Repressed trauma or emotion, when buried too deep for too long, doesn't disappear—it festers. It waits.
When Geun-sae erupts during the birthday party, he is the return of the repressed, the mind's buried agony breaking through polite surfaces. The scene is particularly haunting - not just due to the violent externalization of the repressed mind, but also because it shatters the illusion of order the characters worked so hard to maintain.  

The Final Act: When the Mask Splits 
What makes Parasite brilliant isn't just the twist—it's the psychological implosion. Everyone's mask splits:
•The Kims' fantasy of upward mobility is destroyed.
•The Parks' illusion of safety is violated.
•The power structures collapse, but not toward justice—just more trauma.
Ki-taek, now a fugitive, hides in the very basement that symbolized repression—he becomes the ghost. 
Ki-woo, after surviving a brain injury, fantasizes about buying the house one day. It's a dream, a delusion—the only coping mechanism he has left
Conclusion: Parasite as Psychological Mirror
Parasite is a masterclass in narrative, but more importantly, it's a mirror held up to a world where survival demands performance, and truth is buried under class, shame, and fear. The characters in Parasite don't just act—they adapt, mask, suppress, and eventually unravel.
The brilliance of Bong Joon-ho's vision is that it never diagnoses anyone outright. It doesn't pathologize poverty or villainize wealth—it simply shows what happens when human beings are forced to live behind masks for too long. The breakdown isn't cinematic. It's psychological.
